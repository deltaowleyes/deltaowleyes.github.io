# Call to Action #3 — Jordan's Principle without jurisdictional delays
Editorial draft · Original source: TRC Calls to Action (2015), Call #3
https://nctr.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Calls_to_Action_English2.pdf

**LEAH:**
Boozhoo, and welcome to Delta Owl Eyes. We're continuing our examination of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 94 Calls to Action. Today is number 3: jordan's principle without jurisdictional delays.

When people talk about reconciliation, they often talk about promises. But the Truth and Reconciliation Commission gave Canada specific work to do. This Call is about all levels of government fully implementing Jordan's Principle so First Nations children can receive needed services without being caught between governments.

For Indigenous families, the details matter. Government systems make decisions that can shape everyday life, and those decisions need to be understandable, fair, and accountable. We want to explain the requirement clearly before we evaluate anyone's claim of progress.

**CHRIS:**
As an ally, one thing I'm learning is to distinguish the actual wording of a Call from the much broader political conversation around it. What does this Call require, and what evidence would show that the requirement has been met?

**LEAH:**
**Education.** Call number 3 focuses on all levels of government fully implementing Jordan's Principle so First Nations children can receive needed services without being caught between governments. That is the Commission's recommendation, not a claim by Delta Owl Eyes that a particular government has fulfilled it.

The people affected should be able to understand what is being offered, how decisions are made, and how they can raise concerns. Public discussion also needs to respect differences between First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities rather than treating everyone as one group.

This is why we're building a separate record for each Call. A headline about new funding, a new law, or a new program may be relevant. But we still need to compare the evidence against the exact requirement.

**CHRIS:**
**Hard Truths.** Here's the distinction we need to remember: A service delayed by an argument about which government pays is not a child-centred response.

It's easy for a news report to stop at an announcement. The harder work is returning months or years later and asking what actually changed.

**LEAH:**
And accountability shouldn't be reduced to a contest between optimistic press releases and pessimistic headlines. We need to identify who is responsible, what was promised, whether affected communities were involved, and what outcomes can be documented.

The Commission's recommendations came out of testimony and examination of the residential-school system and its continuing legacy. That history matters when evaluating present-day institutions. It also means our reporting must be careful not to speak for every Indigenous family or community.

At Delta Owl Eyes, we will distinguish the text of the Call, independently reported developments, and our own interpretation. We won't treat a funding commitment as a measured result. We won't describe a Call as completed just because one related action has occurred.

**CHRIS:**
**Accountability.** Here are some questions worth putting to the responsible governments and organizations. Can families obtain timely services? How are delays and denials reviewed? Are governments publishing transparent service outcomes?

Those questions are not accusations. They are a way of finding out whether public commitments have translated into meaningful change.

**LEAH:**
And we want to hear from people with direct experience, without assuming one person's experience represents everyone. We also want to hear from the governments and organizations responsible for implementation, and compare their answers with published evidence.

Our goal is education that leads to better questions, and accountability grounded in facts. When new evidence emerges, we'll report it as a dated update rather than quietly changing the history of what we said.

For now, remember what Call number 3 actually asks for: all levels of government fully implementing Jordan's Principle so First Nations children can receive needed services without being caught between governments. That's the standard against which progress should be measured.
