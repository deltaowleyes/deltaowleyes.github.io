HARD TRUTHS. EDUCATION. ACCOUNTABILITY.

Call 73 — Residential school burial registers

Editorial recording draft — current implementation status requires separate dated verification.

LEAH:

Boozhoo, welcome to Delta Owl Eyes. Today we're looking at Call 73: residential school burial registers.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission asks for the federal government to work with churches, Indigenous communities and former residential school students to establish and maintain an online registry of residential school cemeteries, including maps where possible. We must start with that specific recommendation, rather than a government announcement that may address only part of it.

Call 73 asks for an online registry of residential school cemeteries. That is different from the student death register in Call 72. It should identify cemeteries and include plot maps showing burial locations where possible.

CHRIS:

As an ally, I want to know whether a maintained online cemetery registry exists and how its information is checked. Where can families and communities find it, and how are corrections handled?

LEAH:

Education. The Call names collaboration and an online registry, with maps when possible; sensitivity to families and communities is essential.

The federal government is asked to work with churches, Indigenous communities and former residential school students. All three groups are named in the recommendation, and affected communities must be heard in decisions about sensitive information.

First Nations, Inuit and Métis communities are distinct. Their perspectives cannot be reduced to a single story. We should listen carefully without assuming that one account represents everyone.

The registry must be established and maintained, not merely proposed. Plot maps are requested where possible, so a missing map is not automatically evidence that an institution has ignored the Call.

CHRIS:

Hard Truths. A new program or official statement might be relevant, but it does not automatically meet every requirement. What was actually changed, and can the public verify it?

What evidence shows the registry is being updated? Which partners contributed records, and how are uncertain entries distinguished from confirmed information?

LEAH:

Families and communities deserve respectful treatment when information is published. The need for an accessible record should be considered alongside Indigenous protocols and the privacy of those affected.

The original wording is important: the federal government to work with churches, Indigenous communities and former residential school students to establish and maintain an online registry of residential school cemeteries, including maps where possible. If only one part of that requirement has been addressed, our reporting should say so.

We will not assign a current completion rating without a dated assessment.

CHRIS:

Accountability. Where is the official record of the requested action? Who evaluates the results? What remains unfinished, and who must act next?

Can the responsible institutions document the registry's coverage, update procedures and community involvement? A launch announcement alone is not proof of ongoing maintenance.

LEAH:

We want to hear from Indigenous advocates and people with direct experience while respecting differences among communities. Their knowledge can reveal issues that are not visible in official summaries.

We will publish sources and make clear what is verified and what is our interpretation. When new information emerges, we will provide a dated update rather than quietly rewriting an earlier report.

Call 73 asks for a maintained cemetery registry created collaboratively. Its progress should be assessed against that specific obligation.

CHRIS:

We will keep comparing the original recommendation with documented action. Viewers should be able to check the source for themselves and ask informed questions.

LEAH:

This is Delta Owl Eyes. Hard Truths. Education. Accountability. Miigwech for watching.
