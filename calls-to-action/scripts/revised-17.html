Call to Action #17 — Reclaiming names changed by residential schools

Editorial recording draft — current implementation status requires separate dated verification.

LEAH:

Boozhoo, and welcome to Delta Owl Eyes. Today we're examining Call to Action number 17: reclaiming names changed by residential schools.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission called for all levels of government to waive administrative costs for five years so Survivors and families can reclaim names changed by residential schools and update official identity documents. That is our starting point. A related announcement is not necessarily evidence that the recommendation has been fulfilled.

Language and identity connect generations. Residential schools disrupted families and the transmission of Indigenous languages, names, and cultural knowledge. These Calls address parts of that legacy.

CHRIS:

As an ally, I want to ask questions without speaking for Indigenous communities. If one document can be updated without a fee but another cannot, what remains? Where can families find reliable information?

LEAH:

Education. The Call names birth certificates, passports, driver licences, health cards, status cards and social insurance numbers. The five-year waiver and range of documents matter.

We must also recognize that First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities are distinct. Languages differ, as do local priorities and the relationships communities have with governments.

When reporting on this Call, we should distinguish what the Commission requested, what institutions have done, and what people are experiencing. Those are not interchangeable.

An official announcement may be relevant, but it must be checked against the original requirement. We will not declare a Call completed based on a headline alone.

CHRIS:

Hard Truths. A name carries identity and family connection. Separate government fees and disconnected processes can create barriers even after one office waives a fee.

The public deserves evidence about implementation, not just a promise. And community perspectives can reveal gaps that official summaries miss.

LEAH:

For Indigenous families, this work is about more than a government checklist. It concerns the ability to carry knowledge, language, and identity forward with dignity.

People doing this work need to be heard in decisions about priorities and resources. We should not assume that an institution's description of success is the only valid measure.

We also have to be clear about what we don't know. This episode explains the recommendation. A separate dated report is needed to establish current implementation status.

CHRIS:

Accountability. Which governments offered five-year waivers, when did they begin, and which documents are covered?

These are questions for public institutions and for our reporting, not accusations against individuals.

LEAH:

We will listen to people with direct experience while respecting that no single person speaks for every community.

For Call number 17, remember the precise requirement: all levels of government to waive administrative costs for five years so Survivors and families can reclaim names changed by residential schools and update official identity documents. That is what we must keep checking.

When evidence changes, we'll publish a dated update and explain the source. We won't silently rewrite what was recorded before.

CHRIS:

We should check the responsibility, the official records, and the experience of families navigating multiple agencies. We should also explain which documents are covered. That's how we keep our work accountable: follow the original Call, check the evidence, and ask what remains.

LEAH:

This is Delta Owl Eyes. Hard Truths. Education. Accountability. Miigwech for watching.
