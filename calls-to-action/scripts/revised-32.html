HARD TRUTHS. EDUCATION. ACCOUNTABILITY.

Call 32 — Judicial sentencing discretion

Editorial recording draft — current implementation status requires separate dated verification.

LEAH:

Boozhoo, welcome to Delta Owl Eyes. Today we're examining Call number 32, judicial sentencing discretion.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission called for the federal government to amend the Criminal Code so judges can give reasons for departing from mandatory minimums and conditional-sentence restrictions. We need to understand the exact request, not just the headlines around it.

The Commission's work documented the history and legacy of residential schools. These justice recommendations ask institutions to respond to that history with concrete action.

CHRIS:

As an ally, I want to ask who is responsible, what the original requirement says, and what evidence would show that it has been met. A promise and an outcome are not the same thing.

LEAH:

Education. The Call specifically asks for reasoned judicial discretion.

For Indigenous communities, justice is about more than institutional language. Decisions affect families, access to services, and trust in public systems.

The experiences of First Nations, Inuit and Métis peoples differ. We must not treat one perspective as the voice of everyone, or claim to know the outcome of every case.

When we assess this Call, we should separate the Commission's original wording, official implementation steps, and evidence of actual results.

We will not assign a current completion rating without a separately dated assessment and clear sources. That is important because a relevant announcement may not meet every requirement.

CHRIS:

Hard Truths. A government announcement or policy statement is not proof that the whole recommendation has been implemented. We need dated records, a clear explanation of responsibility, and evidence of what changed.

The public should be able to inspect that evidence and ask questions without needing specialized legal training.

LEAH:

For communities, accountability also means being heard when decisions are designed and reviewed. Consultation is not the same as community authority, and an institution's account is not the only perspective.

We want to hear from Indigenous legal advocates and people with direct experience, while respecting privacy and the differences between communities.

The important thing is to keep returning to the original requirement: the federal government to amend the Criminal Code so judges can give reasons for departing from mandatory minimums and conditional-sentence restrictions. Each part deserves its own evidence.

CHRIS:

Accountability. Where is the written record? Who is responsible for the next action? What independent evaluation exists, and what remains unresolved?

We should compare official documents with the Commission's actual words. If sources conflict, we should explain the disagreement rather than present a guess as fact.

LEAH:

This series is an invitation to learn and to ask more precise questions. It is not a substitute for legal advice, and we will distinguish interpretation from verified reporting.

When new information appears, we'll publish a dated update. We won't quietly rewrite an older claim.

The goal is meaningful accountability rooted in evidence and respect for Indigenous voices.

CHRIS:

We'll provide the original source so viewers can verify the Call for themselves. We'll also explain what we still need to investigate before making claims about progress.

LEAH:

This is Delta Owl Eyes. Hard Truths. Education. Accountability. Miigwech for watching.
