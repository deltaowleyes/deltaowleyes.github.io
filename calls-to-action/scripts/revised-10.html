HARD TRUTHS. EDUCATION. ACCOUNTABILITY.

Call to Action #10 — Education legislation developed with Indigenous peoples

Editorial recording draft — timing, speaker balance and current implementation status require final verification.

LEAH:

Boozhoo, and welcome to Delta Owl Eyes. Today we're examining Call to Action number 10: education legislation developed with indigenous peoples.

This recommendation asks for new Aboriginal education legislation drafted with the full participation and informed consent of Indigenous peoples, incorporating sufficient funding, culturally appropriate curricula, language protection, parental and community responsibility, and accountability. We need to start with that precise request, because a news headline about a program or an announcement doesn't necessarily tell us whether the recommendation has been fulfilled.

For Indigenous families and communities, education is not just a set of statistics. It is connected to language, culture, opportunity, and the ability to make decisions about our futures. Experiences differ among First Nations, Inuit, and Métis people, and no single speaker can represent everyone.

CHRIS:

As an ally, I want to understand what the Commission actually asked governments to do. How can viewers distinguish a promising initiative from measurable progress on this specific Call?

LEAH:

Education. Call number 10 addresses new Aboriginal education legislation drafted with the full participation and informed consent of Indigenous peoples, incorporating sufficient funding, culturally appropriate curricula, language protection, parental and community responsibility, and accountability. Those words set the standard for accountability.

It helps to distinguish three different things: a government promise, the actions taken to carry it out, and the results experienced by people. Each requires its own evidence.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission documented how residential schools harmed Indigenous children, families, cultures, and communities. The education Calls respond to that history. They ask institutions to take responsibility for changing systems, not simply to expect Indigenous people to overcome barriers alone.

We also have to respect Indigenous decision-making. A program designed for a community is not automatically the same as a program designed with that community. When governments claim progress, they should explain who participated and how decisions were made.

CHRIS:

Hard Truths. Consultation is not automatically the same as informed consent. A law's title does not prove its design respects Indigenous authority.

A budget announcement or policy document might be a useful starting point. It is not a substitute for outcomes that can be checked.

LEAH:

That distinction is especially important when reporting on education. A national number may hide substantial differences between communities. A new program might help some families while leaving others without access.

We will not label this Call complete because of a related announcement, and we won't present an interpretation as a verified fact. Any current completion rating needs its own dated evidence and an identified methodology.

CHRIS:

Accountability. Which Indigenous governments participated and consented? How would the law protect languages and community control? Is funding sufficient and predictable?

Those are questions to investigate with official records, independent research, and community perspectives. They aren't allegations that any particular organization has failed.

LEAH:

We should also ask whether the people most affected can see the information, challenge it, and participate in decisions about what happens next.

When we cover a new development, we'll tell you the date, the source, the exact Call it relates to, and what remains uncertain. If a later development changes the picture, it deserves a new report rather than a silent rewrite of what we recorded before.

The goal is not to turn reconciliation into a scoreboard. It is to make promises understandable and outcomes accountable.

For Call number 10, remember the central requirement: new Aboriginal education legislation drafted with the full participation and informed consent of Indigenous peoples, incorporating sufficient funding, culturally appropriate curricula, language protection, parental and community responsibility, and accountability. That is what our reporting must keep returning to.

CHRIS:

This is Delta Owl Eyes.

LEAH:

Hard Truths. Education. Accountability. Miigwech for watching.
