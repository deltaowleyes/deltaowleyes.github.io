Call to Action #1 — Keeping Indigenous Children and Families Together

Editorial recording draft — not yet validated for timing, speaker ratio, or current implementation status.

LEAH:

Boozhoo. Welcome to Delta Owl Eyes. Today we're beginning with the first of Canada's 94 Calls to Action. Call number one is about Indigenous children, their families, and the responsibility to reduce the number of Indigenous children in care.

For many families, this isn't an abstract policy discussion. It concerns who gets to raise children, whether families receive help before a crisis, and whether children can stay connected to their communities and cultures.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission didn't ask for one small adjustment. It called on federal, provincial, territorial, and Indigenous governments to commit to reducing the number of Indigenous children in care, with five specific actions.

CHRIS:

One of those actions is monitoring and assessing investigations of neglect. Another is making sure child-welfare workers understand the history and continuing impacts of residential schools.

LEAH:

Education. The Call also asks governments to provide adequate resources so Indigenous communities and child-welfare organizations can keep families together when it is safe to do so.

It calls for culturally appropriate environments for children, wherever they live. And it says investigators must understand that Indigenous families and communities may be able to provide better solutions for family healing.

Finally, people making child-welfare decisions must consider how residential schools affected children and caregivers.

Those requirements belong together. Training without resources is not enough. Resources without meaningful community involvement are not enough either.

CHRIS:

Hard Truths. We can't measure success only by how many new programs governments announce. The central question is whether fewer Indigenous children are entering care, and whether families have safe, culturally appropriate support.

LEAH:

A child-welfare system should not treat a family's need for practical support as something separate from the historical policies that disrupted Indigenous families.

That's why this Call requires more than good intentions. It requires decisions informed by history, support for family and community solutions, and evidence that the number of children in care is actually declining.

CHRIS:

Accountability. What information should governments publish so the public can judge progress? Are investigations being monitored? Are staff receiving meaningful training? Are families getting help before separation becomes necessary?

LEAH:

And who gets to define whether a service is culturally appropriate? Are Indigenous governments and communities able to shape those services? Do public reports show outcomes over time, not just spending?

These questions are important because children and families are more than statistics. Accountability means listening to the people affected while also demanding clear, comparable evidence.

For our viewers, here's the takeaway: Call number one is a commitment to reduce the number of Indigenous children in care through better oversight, adequate support, historical understanding, and culturally appropriate decisions.

We'll keep examining what governments have done, what independent sources can confirm, and what still needs answers.

CHRIS:

As an ally, I want to know whether those five requirements are measured separately. A decline in the number of children in care matters, but so do the resources that help families stay safely together.

CHRIS:

We also need to be careful with our reporting. This script explains what the Commission asked for. It does not claim that Call number one is complete, or assign a current progress rating without a separately dated assessment.

CHRIS:

This is Delta Owl Eyes.

LEAH:

Hard Truths. Education. Accountability. Miigwech for watching.
