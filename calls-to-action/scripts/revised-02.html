Call to Action #2 — Publishing the child-welfare numbers

Editorial recording draft — not yet validated for timing, speaker ratio, or current implementation status.

LEAH:

Boozhoo, and welcome to Delta Owl Eyes. We're continuing our examination of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 94 Calls to Action. Today is number 2: publishing the child-welfare numbers. When people talk about reconciliation, they often talk about promises. But the Truth and Reconciliation Commission gave Canada specific work to do. This Call is about annual public reporting about Indigenous children in care, comparisons with non-Indigenous children, reasons children are taken into care, prevention and care spending, and whether interventions work. For Indigenous families, the details matter. Government systems make decisions that can shape everyday life, and those decisions need to be understandable, fair, and accountable. We want to explain the requirement clearly before we evaluate anyone's claim of progress.

CHRIS:

As an ally, one thing I'm learning is to distinguish the actual wording of a Call from the much broader political conversation around it. What does this Call require, and what evidence would show that the requirement has been met?

LEAH:

Education. Call number 2 focuses on annual public reporting about Indigenous children in care, comparisons with non-Indigenous children, reasons children are taken into care, prevention and care spending, and whether interventions work. That is the Commission's recommendation, not a claim by Delta Owl Eyes that a particular government has fulfilled it. The people affected should be able to understand what is being offered, how decisions are made, and how they can raise concerns. Public discussion also needs to respect differences between First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities rather than treating everyone as one group. This is why we're building a separate record for each Call. A headline about new funding, a new law, or a new program may be relevant. But we still need to compare the evidence against the exact requirement.

CHRIS:

Hard Truths. Here's the distinction we need to remember: Annual reports are not just spreadsheets; they let families and communities ask whether policies are improving outcomes. It's easy for a news report to stop at an announcement. The harder work is returning months or years later and asking what actually changed.

LEAH:

And accountability shouldn't be reduced to a contest between optimistic press releases and pessimistic headlines. We need to identify who is responsible, what was promised, whether affected communities were involved, and what outcomes can be documented. The Commission's recommendations came out of testimony and examination of the residential-school system and its continuing legacy. That history matters when evaluating present-day institutions. It also means our reporting must be careful not to speak for every Indigenous family or community. At Delta Owl Eyes, we will distinguish the text of the Call, independently reported developments, and our own interpretation. We won't treat a funding commitment as a measured result. We won't describe a Call as completed just because one related action has occurred.

CHRIS:

Accountability. Here are some questions worth putting to the responsible governments and organizations. Where are the latest comparable figures? Do reports separate First Nations, Inuit and Métis data appropriately? Are prevention spending and results published together? Those questions are not accusations. They are a way of finding out whether public commitments have translated into meaningful change.

LEAH:

And we want to hear from people with direct experience, without assuming one person's experience represents everyone. We also want to hear from the governments and organizations responsible for implementation, and compare their answers with published evidence. Our goal is education that leads to better questions, and accountability grounded in facts. When new evidence emerges, we'll report it as a dated update rather than quietly changing the history of what we said. For now, remember what Call number 2 actually asks for: annual public reporting about Indigenous children in care, comparisons with non-Indigenous children, reasons children are taken into care, prevention and care spending, and whether interventions work. That's the standard against which progress should be measured.

CHRIS:

This is Delta Owl Eyes.

LEAH:

Hard Truths. Education. Accountability. Miigwech for watching.
