HARD TRUTHS. EDUCATION. ACCOUNTABILITY.

Call 80 — A National Day for Truth and Reconciliation

Editorial recording draft — current implementation status requires separate dated verification.

LEAH:

Boozhoo, welcome to Delta Owl Eyes. Today we're looking at Call 80: a national day for truth and reconciliation.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission asks for the federal government, collaborating with Indigenous peoples, to establish a statutory National Day for Truth and Reconciliation honouring Survivors, their families and communities. We must start with that specific recommendation, rather than a government announcement that may address only part of it.

The residential-school system and its legacy are central to the Commission's work. These Calls ask institutions to confront that history and make meaningful changes.

CHRIS:

As an ally, I want to understand who is responsible, which official records show action, and what Indigenous communities say about the results. We should not confuse a promise with a demonstrated outcome.

LEAH:

Education. The Call specifies a statutory holiday for public commemoration. Any current progress claim needs dated evidence.

For Indigenous families, these decisions can affect community relationships, access to knowledge and trust in public institutions. Those experiences deserve attention alongside legal and policy documents.

First Nations, Inuit and Métis communities are distinct. Their perspectives cannot be reduced to a single story. We should listen carefully without assuming that one account represents everyone.

To assess this Call, we need to distinguish the Commission's request, the steps taken to implement it, and evidence about the outcomes. Each question needs its own sources.

CHRIS:

Hard Truths. A new program or official statement might be relevant, but it does not automatically meet every requirement. What was actually changed, and can the public verify it?

We should examine the dates of the evidence and the responsibilities of different institutions. If sources disagree, we need to explain that uncertainty rather than guessing.

LEAH:

Reconciliation requires institutions to listen to people affected by their decisions. Communities should have meaningful opportunities to shape programs and evaluate whether they work.

The original wording is important: the federal government, collaborating with Indigenous peoples, to establish a statutory National Day for Truth and Reconciliation honouring Survivors, their families and communities. If only one part of that requirement has been addressed, our reporting should say so.

We will not assign a current completion rating without a dated assessment.

CHRIS:

Accountability. Where is the official record of the requested action? Who evaluates the results? What remains unfinished, and who must act next?

I also want to know whether Indigenous communities participated in the decision and whether their concerns changed the outcome. Those are fair questions for institutions, not accusations against individuals.

LEAH:

We want to hear from Indigenous advocates and people with direct experience while respecting differences among communities. Their knowledge can reveal issues that are not visible in official summaries.

We will publish sources and make clear what is verified and what is our interpretation. When new information emerges, we will provide a dated update rather than quietly rewriting an earlier report.

Call 80 deserves its own assessment. Its specific requirements should not disappear behind broad promises about reconciliation.

CHRIS:

We will keep comparing the original recommendation with documented action. Viewers should be able to check the source for themselves and ask informed questions.

LEAH:

This is Delta Owl Eyes. Hard Truths. Education. Accountability. Miigwech for watching.
