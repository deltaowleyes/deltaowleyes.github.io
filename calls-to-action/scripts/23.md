# Call to Action #23 — Indigenous health professionals and cultural competency
Editorial draft · TRC Calls to Action (2015), #23
https://nctr.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Calls_to_Action_English2.pdf

**LEAH:**
Boozhoo, and welcome to Delta Owl Eyes. Today we're examining Call to Action number 23: indigenous health professionals and cultural competency.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission asks for all levels of government to increase Indigenous health-care professionals, retain Indigenous providers in Indigenous communities and provide cultural competency training for all health-care professionals. We need to begin with those responsibilities, not with a headline about a related initiative.

For Indigenous families, health is connected to community, history, relationships and access to respectful services. People have different experiences, and no single person speaks for all First Nations, Inuit and Métis communities.

**CHRIS:**
I'm approaching this as an ally. How many professionals enter and remain in the workforce? What evidence demonstrates training changes professional practice?

**LEAH:**
**Education.** The recommendation has three separate parts: increasing numbers, supporting retention, and training professionals. One measure cannot substitute for the others.

Residential schools and other government policies affected generations of families. Understanding that history helps explain why the Commission placed health among its Calls to Action.

We also need to be precise. The original Call identifies a particular responsibility. A government statement, a new program and a measurable result are different kinds of evidence.

The people most affected should help shape what success means. Their perspectives matter alongside public records, research and the experience of health professionals.

**CHRIS:**
**Hard Truths.** Recruitment without retention leaves communities facing recurring gaps. Training should also translate into respectful care, not just a certificate.

The public needs to know what was promised, what has been implemented and what still needs attention. We should also check the dates of our sources before making a claim about current progress.

**LEAH:**
That is why our reporting won't label this Call complete simply because a related announcement exists. We'll distinguish the Commission's words from later actions and from our interpretation.

Respectful health care should not depend on someone knowing how to navigate several layers of bureaucracy. Systems need to explain their responsibilities clearly.

And when communities identify priorities, institutions should explain how those voices influence decisions. Consultation is meaningful only when people can see what happened afterward.

**CHRIS:**
**Accountability.** What recruitment and retention measures exist? How is training provided and its effect evaluated?

These are questions for our reporting and for responsible institutions. They aren't accusations against an individual health worker. We will ask for records and community perspectives rather than guessing.

**LEAH:**
We'll look for dated official sources, independent evidence and community perspectives. Where information is missing or outdated, we'll say so instead of guessing.

Call number 23 is specifically about indigenous health professionals and cultural competency. Its requirements deserve to be assessed on their own terms.

This series is not medical advice. It's a way to understand the Commission's recommendations and the public responsibilities they create.

**CHRIS:**
We'll keep checking the original recommendation against the evidence. When something changes, we'll publish a dated update.

**LEAH:**
This is Delta Owl Eyes. Hard Truths. Education. Accountability. Miigwech for watching.
